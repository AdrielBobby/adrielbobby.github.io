02 / The problem

Learning rhythm needs a teacher in the room.

The chenda is learned by ear and by feel, from a teacher who hears you and corrects you. The team wanted a way to practise any track without one: strike the air with your fingers, feel the tempo, see which finger is next, and get coached afterwards.

03 / What was built

Sense, think, act.

A glove with an IMU, a vibration motor and an LED on each finger, connected to a Raspberry Pi 5 and a live dashboard.

Sense

MPU6050 on the finger detects strikes (peak against rest, 120 ms refractory). Laptop keys stand in for the other fingers with the same strike event.

Think

Timing math judges every hit in under a millisecond. Gemma hears a track and decides its structure, finger map and coaching.

Act

Five buzzers and five LEDs on the Pi's GPIO, browser and Pi audio, and a dashboard with falling notes and live judging.

04 / Key decisions

What the model does, and what it doesn't.

  1. Judge timing with math, not the model

    Every hit is scored by deterministic code. Gemma is kept for what math can't do: hearing a track, deciding its cycle and finger mapping, coaching and composing drills.

  2. Keep Malayalam coaching deterministic

    No offline Malayalam speech exists, and a small model gets the language wrong. The Malayalam text is generated by code, so it is accurate; Gemma writes English and Manglish.

  3. Two Gemma engines, side by side

    A larger Gemma 3n runs on a laptop GPU over an SSH tunnel, and Gemma 2 2B runs fully on the Pi. A dashboard card switches between them and shows timings. A smaller 3n variant ran out of memory on the Pi's 4 GB and is documented.
